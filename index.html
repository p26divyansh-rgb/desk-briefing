Analyst notes

What's changed since you last read your notes, and two places where the wiring doesn't quite match the data.

Stale — Warsh is now Fed Chair, not a speaker

Kevin Warsh is the sitting Fed Chair. His hawkish Jackson Hole speech (28 Aug) moved September hike odds from ~55% to ~60% — and the Fed followed through: 16 Sep, +25bp to 3.75–4.00%, first hike since 2023, dot plot pointing to one more this year. This is now your best live example of the "hawkish speech → markets" mechanism (§2), because you can narrate what actually happened rather than the theory alone.

Two different bond stories were merged into one

The US Treasury tripled its long-bond buyback size (to $6bn/operation, 10–30Y sector) — a liquidity-support operation funded by new issuance, not "reducing debt," driven by fading foreign demand (China and Japan both trimming UST holdings). The US 30Y sits near a 19-year high, ~5.2–5.3% — nowhere close to 7%. The "sub-7%" figure in your notes is almost certainly India's 10Y G-sec (§3.1), which the RBI is fighting to hold under 7% via OMO sales — it just broke that level. Keep the three levers separate: Treasury (issuance/liquidity), Fed (policy rate), RBI (defending a yield).

Oil's direction is right, the driver is inverted

Brent at $104.45 is a geopolitical supply-disruption story (Middle East attacks on facilities and tankers, >10mb/d of Gulf output shut in) — not an OPEC+ cut. If anything, OPEC and the IEA had been cutting 2026 demand outlooks going in, i.e. fundamentals pointed to oversupply. Your Russia-output and Venezuela figures don't show up in current reporting — don't cite them as hard numbers; lead with the Middle East disruption story, which is well documented.

Gold and silver already corrected — use this as your worked example

Both are down hard from January 2026 peaks: gold $5,589 → $4,298 (−23%), silver $121.62 → $64.2 (−47%). Proximate cause: the same Warsh/Fed hawkish turn above. The structural bid (de-dollarization, central-bank buying) is intact underneath — the trade right now is "a rate shock overwhelmed the structural bid," not "these are still ripping."

02

The transmission mechanism

How one hawkish Fed speech moves rates, gold, the dollar, and the rupee — worked through the actual Warsh episode.

The chain

  1. Speech signals hawkishness → fed-funds futures reprice hike odds up (Warsh's speech: ~55% → ~60% for September).
  2. Higher expected policy rate → higher real yields — the market now expects tighter policy for longer.
  3. Gold and silver fall. Both are non-yielding — their opportunity cost is the real rate you forgo by holding them instead of a bond. Real yields up → that cost rises → they get sold. This is exactly what happened.
  4. The dollar strengthens — higher US rates pull capital into dollar assets (interest-rate-parity logic).
  5. EM currencies weaken, rupee included — the rate gap narrows or reverses, and a firmer dollar mechanically weakens every currency quoted against it.
  6. Imported inflation rises for EM importers — a weaker rupee makes India's oil and gold imports costlier in rupee terms, feeding CPI.
  7. Inflation expectations anchor faster — a central bank seen as willing to hike moves the curve without necessarily hiking as much in practice. That's the entire point of hawkish jawboning.

Say this out loud version: "A hawkish Fed repricing raises real yields, which raises the opportunity cost of holding gold and silver, strengthens the dollar, and pressures EM currencies and imported inflation — we just watched that whole channel play out between late August and mid-September."

03

India rates · fx · flows

Structurally strong, cyclically squeezed — rates, fiscal, sector policy, flows, and two trade ideas with hedges.

3.1 Rates & the FCNR(B) story

RBI opened a special FCNR(B) dollar-swap window (8 Jun–30 Sep 2026), letting NRIs earn 5.5–7% tax-free on 3–5yr USD deposits, with RBI swapping the dollars for rupees. Sized for ~$50bn — banks brought in ~$133bn. Mechanism: NRI deposits dollars → bank swaps with RBI for rupees at a forward rate → RBI's reserves get a shot in the arm (they'd fallen from a $728bn Feb peak to ~$682bn) → RBI can lean against rupee weakness without burning existing reserves.

The catch: $133bn swapped into rupees is a large liquidity injection. To keep it from being inflationary, RBI has had to drain it back out — ~₹1 trillion of OMO bond sales through September. That selling, stacked on the oil-driven yield sell-off, is why the 10Y G-sec just broke 7% for the first time in three months.

3.2 Government borrowing & the crowding-out channel today's news

The Centre just cut its FY27 gross market borrowing to ₹15.99 lakh crore, down from the Budget Estimate of ₹17.20 lakh crore — a ₹1.2 lakh crore reduction. H2 FY27 borrowing via dated securities: ₹7.86 lakh crore across 23 weekly auctions (including ₹15,000cr sovereign green bonds), with a higher share of ultra-long paper (15Y up from 14.5% to 17.6% of issuance, plus more 30/40/50Y). Driver: stronger-than-budgeted revenue — tax revenue of ₹8.45 lakh crore and non-tax revenue of ₹4.23 lakh crore in the first four months of FY27, lifted by a large RBI dividend transfer (₹2.87 lakh crore, up from ₹2.69 lakh crore) — plus a bond-switch operation that cut the FY27 repayment need to ₹4.36 trillion from a budgeted ₹5.47 trillion. FY27 fiscal deficit target: 4.3% of GDP, down from FY26's revised 4.4%.

Why "government borrows less" is good — the mechanism, precisely: government finances its deficit by issuing G-secs, which compete with corporate bonds and bank credit for the same pool of savings. To place a large borrowing programme, it has to offer yields attractive enough to draw that much capital — pulling up the whole rate structure, since G-sec yields are the risk-free base every other loan and bond in the economy is priced off. Higher risk-free yields make private borrowing costlier — directly (corporate bonds priced as a spread over G-secs) and indirectly (banks find risk-free G-secs more attractive than lending to companies, "crowding out" credit that would otherwise go to private capex). A smaller borrowing calendar runs this in reverse — less competition for savings, easier yields, cheaper private financing — which is exactly why bond markets rallied on this news.

Nuance worth having ready: crowding-out is strongest when the economy is near full capacity and savings are scarce. When there's slack — spare savings, soft private credit demand — government borrowing can be absorbed without much yield impact, and public capex can even crowd in private investment (a highway or transmission line unlocking capex around it). So "less borrowing is unambiguously good" is directionally right but not a universal law.

The rupee link — the honest version: the direct classical channel is the twin-deficit idea: a lower fiscal deficit reduces the government's net dis-saving, improves headline fiscal metrics, and lowers the sovereign risk premium — generally currency-supportive through credibility, and through crowding in private investment that eventually supports growth and exports. But the mechanical link is weaker for India than for economies that fund deficits externally, because India's G-sec market is overwhelmingly domestically funded (banks, insurers, EPFO, RBI) — smaller borrowing mainly means less competition for domestic savings, not less external borrowing. There's even a partial offset: less G-sec supply and lower yields make Indian bonds slightly less attractive to yield-seeking FPI debt investors (lower carry) — so on the margin, smaller borrowing could modestly reduce, not increase, FPI debt inflows. Net answer: fiscal consolidation supports the rupee mainly via credibility and growth, not a straight mechanical line — and it's genuinely two-sided once you account for carry.

Trader's point to make unprompted: the G-sec curve has two forces pulling opposite ways right now — RBI selling bonds via OMO (drains liquidity, pushes yields up) against the government issuing fewer bonds this year (less supply, pushes yields down). That tension, not a single clean narrative, is the actual state of the India rates market today.

3.3 Fertilizer & urea — from import dependence to self-reliance

India is the world's largest urea importer, sourcing roughly a quarter of its annual requirement from overseas — which is exactly what turned this year's Strait of Hormuz shock (§below) into a fiscal event rather than a footnote. On 15 July 2026 the Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs approved the National Investment Policy for Urea 2026 (NIPU-2026), a structural response aimed at cutting that import dependence permanently, not just riding out this year's spike.

  • The policy has already drawn ~₹2.04 trillion (~$23bn) in investment proposals — 17 of them, from GAIL, Hindustan Urvarak & Rasayan (HURL), Kribhco and others — covering 8–9 new plants plus expansions of existing capacity.
  • Design improvements vs. the old 2012 framework: fixed and variable costs are now separated for transparency; a bounded return-on-equity band (12–16%) replaces open-ended subsidy exposure; and — the detail that matters most to an investor — fixed costs convert into rupee terms after four years at the prevailing exchange rate, which is a direct fix for the FX risk that made the 2012 policy unattractive to new capacity.
  • The two-speed story to tell in the room: this year had a cyclical fertilizer chapter (the Hormuz-driven price and subsidy shock, already covered in §3.4, now easing) and NIPU-2026 is the structural chapter — a multi-year push to stop India being exposed to exactly that kind of shock again. Being able to separate the cyclical shock from the structural policy response is a noticeably better answer than treating "fertilizer" as one undifferentiated story.

3.4 Inflation — what's driving the 4.82% print

  • Weak monsoon — rainfall deficit hit ~42% below normal in June, easing to ~15% by September; on track for the weakest monsoon since 2009. Reservoirs fell to ~28% of capacity, pushing farmers onto groundwater/electric irrigation pumps — the direct link from "bad monsoon" to "higher power demand." Economists estimate a weak monsoon can add up to 100bp to CPI.
  • Sugar — India expected a surplus and started exporting; red rot disease hit UP cane fields, cutting production estimates from ~343 LMT to ~306 LMT. India reversed course, now allowing 1 million tonnes of duty-free raw sugar imports (normally 100% duty) through 31 Oct. Retail sugar rose ~16% in a month (₹48.18 → ₹55.70/kg, Jul–Aug).
  • Wheat — the opposite story: record output (120.65 MT) and 5-year-high FCI stocks (50.5 MT) let India lift its 4-year wheat export ban in August to support farmgate prices — disinflationary for wheat, doesn't offset food broadly.
  • Fertilizer/urea — the acute 2026 shock: the Strait of Hormuz crisis briefly cut India's urea output ~25% and pushed import prices toward $935–959/tonne, nearly doubling the projected subsidy bill toward ~₹3.4 lakh crore; it's since eased (Hormuz reopened, urea back near $415–420/tonne) toward ~₹2.5 lakh crore. See §3.3 for the structural policy response this triggered.

3.5 Exports & import dependence

Gems & jewellery: FY26 exports ~$27.7bn, stable despite a US slowdown, helped by a US–India trade-framework agreement. Composition: cut & polished diamonds 43.9%, gold jewellery 41.0%, silver jewellery 5.3%. India cuts and polishes diamonds it doesn't mine — rough stones are imported (~$700m in June alone) — a pure re-export/value-add trade, acutely sensitive to global demand for polished stones and to rough-diamond supply upstream. Best answer if asked which export sector is most fragile to a global supply-chain shock.

3.6 UPI MDR — the new merchant fee

Correction to flag for yourself first: the new fee applies to P2M (person-to-merchant) UPI payments only — not P2P. Every peer-to-peer UPI transfer stays 100% free regardless of amount; don't say "P2P" in the room.

From 15 October 2026, NPCI's new rule applies a 0.4% MDR (merchant discount rate) to eligible P2M UPI transactions above ₹2,000, capped at ₹300 for transactions of ₹75,000 and above (worked examples cited in reporting: ~₹12 on a ₹3,000 payment, ~₹200 on ₹50,000). Small merchants under the P2PM framework taking up to ₹1 lakh/month via UPI QR remain fully exempt — zero MDR, protecting kirana-scale merchants. Consumers pay nothing either way; the fee sits with the merchant.

Why this belongs in a markets briefing: UPI has run on zero MDR since 2020, with payment companies compensated via a government incentive scheme instead of merchant fees — a policy-subsidised, effectively zero-revenue payments rail. This reopens a genuine fee-revenue line for banks and payment aggregators in the ecosystem for the first time in years. It's also a small but clean example of the same fiscal-discipline thread running through this whole India section (smaller government borrowing, tighter subsidy design in urea) — recouping part of the cost of running a public payments rail from the transactions that use it, rather than an open-ended subsidy.

3.7 NSE's own listing — why the debut was muted

The National Stock Exchange of India listed on 24 September 2026 at ₹1,800 — just ~1% above the IPO's upper price band of ₹1,785 — despite the issue being subscribed 5.71 times. Bidding ran 17–21 September; the offer was entirely an Offer For Sale (OFS) of 12.64 crore shares, raising ₹21,486–22,562 crore, one of the largest IPOs in Indian history.

Your instinct — size and magnitude — is a real part of the answer, but not the whole answer:

  • Pure OFS, no fresh capital: every rupee raised went to existing shareholders selling down, not into NSE's own balance sheet — there's no new-growth-capital story to re-rate around at listing, only an ownership change among already-large holders.
  • Size: at ~₹21,500cr+, absorbing the stock at the margin needs very deep pockets — strong headline subscription (5.71x) can still coexist with a flat listing once large anchor/institutional allocations are already locked in and the marginal secondary-market buyer is thin.
  • Years of prior unlisted trading (plausible, not confirmed): NSE shares had traded informally in India's unlisted/grey market for years during its long-delayed IPO saga — a real possibility is that much of the "this is a rare, monopoly-like asset" re-rating had already happened before the formal listing, leaving less of a pop for listing day itself. Flag this one as your own reasoning, not a cited fact, if asked to defend it.

Why: (1) India has almost no seat at the AI/data-centre/semiconductor table driving the US, Taiwan and Korea higher — called an "AI loser" this cycle; (2) higher US rates and a firmer post-Warsh dollar pull capital back to the US and out of "relatively expensive" EM; (3) even the money that came back in Jul–Aug went disproportionately into IPOs/primary issuance rather than secondary buying of existing names — a vote for specific new supply, not for India Inc.'s existing valuations. A strong IPO pipeline despite a soft secondary market fits: promoters and PE/VC sponsors use the window to exit at any workable price, and primary demand holds even where index-level conviction hasn't.

3.9 Trade idea — power sector

Long power-financing / infrastructure

Thesis: AI data-centre buildout is power-hungry — India's data-centre capacity has gone from ~375MW (2020) to ~1.57GW (Aug 2026), headed toward ~8GW by 2030, with data-centre-linked demand estimated to add 13–26GW to national power demand by FY32. A multi-year, financeable capex cycle across generation, transmission and distribution.

Primary proxy

Power Finance Corporation (PFC) — doesn't generate power, it finances the sector's capex across generation, transmission, distribution and renewables: a levered, cheap way to play sector-wide capex rather than one generator. ~6.7x P/E — cheap for a structural, multi-year demand story, largely because the market still prices it as a legacy PSU lender rather than an AI-infrastructure financier.

Diversifying proxy

Power Grid Corporation (transmission monopoly, ~3.4% yield — the toll-booth on all new capacity) or NTPC (largest generator, ~75GW, safest long-duration bet). Adani Power is the highest-beta pure generation play for thermal-capacity growth.

Hedge

PFC is effectively a rate-sensitive NBFC — long-duration lending funded partly by market borrowing, so real duration/spread risk if rates rise further (a live risk now, see §3.1). Hedge the rate leg with a short in Indian rate-sensitive financials generally, or an outright short in India 10Y futures/OIS, isolating "power capex" from "rates go up, funding costs rise." Hedge sector risk (a tariff order, a coal-supply shock) by sizing as a basket (PFC + Power Grid) rather than one name.

Risk to flag unprompted

Data-centre demand figures are still FY32 forecasts, and PSU-linked financials carry policy risk (subsidised tariffs, priority-sector lending directives) a pure private-sector trade wouldn't.

3.10 Trade idea — metals

Long India steel/metals, hedged for China spillover

Thesis: India's basic-metals output is expected to grow ~8.7% in 2026, steel demand ~9% (vs. ~1.3% globally) — driven by EV, power infrastructure, data-centres and capex generally. Nifty Metal names (Tata Steel, Hindalco, Nalco) have rallied on it.

Market risk

A global growth slowdown reduces industrial-metal demand broadly — beta shared with every cyclical.

Systematic risk — the China link

China's basic-metals output growth is slowing to ~2.5% as its own steel supply outpaces weak domestic demand — a structural incentive to export the oversupply cheaply ("dumping"), undercutting Indian pricing power. India already uses a provisional safeguard duty to cut Chinese steel imports (China's share fell from 1.11mt to 0.50mt, Jan–Apr 2025) — so the trade partly bets India's trade-protection regime holds. If it's rolled back, or China escalates dumping, the domestic price premium this trade depends on compresses fast.

Hedge

Pair the long India-metals position with a short in a China-proxy metals name or index — isolating "India-specific demand growth" from "global metals cycle," since a China-driven price collapse hits both legs. Alternatively hedge via steel/iron-ore futures, shorting tonnage-equivalent exposure to offset a China-oversupply shock.

3.11 Gold & silver — India angle

Gold's structural bid (central-bank buying, de-dollarization) plus a weak rupee (import cost in INR terms rises even when the dollar price is flat or falling) is a big part of why gold has been a crowded trade for Indian retail/HNI money — and part of why that money hasn't gone into equities instead. Rupee weakness and dollar gold prices can offset each other, so gold-in-rupees can hold up even while gold-in-dollars corrects, as it is right now (see §7.2).

04

China exports · debt · fx

Record surplus, weak foundations — the economic rationale, the LFP/rare-earth angle, and the currency call.

4.1 Why export-led growth without domestic demand is a problem

China closed 2025 with a record $1.19 trillion trade surplus, exports contributing roughly a third of 2025 GDP growth — the highest share since 1997. Sounds like strength; the evidence says it's masking weakness.

  1. The surplus is mostly "we're buying very little," not "we're selling a lot." Imports have stayed roughly flat — Chinese households aren't spending. A trade surplus is exports minus imports; right now it's being driven by the import side.
  2. An economy that can't sell to itself depends on the rest of the world buying from it — fragile, because it's hostage to other countries' demand and their patience with the matching deficit. That dependence is exactly why tariff retaliation and safeguard duties are springing up everywhere (India's steel duties, EV/battery frictions).
  3. The currency mechanism: a persistent, large surplus is a fundamental force pushing the currency up — more foreign buyers need yuan than Chinese buyers need dollars. A stronger yuan makes exports less competitive; in a well-functioning system this is self-correcting (surplus → currency appreciates → exports get pricier → surplus shrinks). China has historically leaned against this via intervention and capital controls, but 2026 forecasts (§4.3) show analysts now expecting the PBoC to tolerate more appreciation — a firmer yuan also offsets imported inflation and supports the "strong currency, strong economy" narrative Beijing wants internationally.
  4. Overproduction + weak domestic demand = deflation, not just a trade statistic. Factories keep running at export scale while nobody at home buys, so prices fall domestically too — ten straight quarters of deflationary pressure, compounding a multi-year property collapse (home prices down 4.5 years, comparable in depth to the US 2008 crash) and cratered consumer confidence.

4.2 LFP batteries & rare earths — the auto-sector angle

  • China controls 94% of global LFP cathode capacity and ~70% of refined lithium supply. LFP is the cost-efficient, safer chemistry now dominant in mass-market EVs — and cathode production technology is exactly what China restricted (export-licensing from Nov 2025), alongside a mid-2025 tightening on rare-earth magnet exports.
  • Real impact: US automakers, including a $3bn Ford LFP plant built around licensed CATL technology, now face uncertain prospects because the underlying tech licence sits at Chinese government discretion.
  • Why it matters: battery cost is the single largest line item in EV manufacturing, and China's grip on both the cathode chemistry and the rare-earth magnets in EV motors lets it throttle non-Chinese EV supply chains at two separate points — genuine structural leverage, not just a trade dispute.
  • Caveat for the room: China has intermittently suspended parts of these controls (a Nov 2025–Nov 2026 suspension window on some items) — treat it as an escalate-and-suspend bargaining tool, not a blanket ban.

4.4 China's own debt problem

China's debt overhang is real but structured differently from the US Treasury story — mostly local-government (LGFV) hidden debt. Beijing has run large-scale local-government bond-swap programs (~CNY 10–12 trillion, 2026 special-refinancing issuance already underway) converting opaque, high-cost local financing-vehicle debt into transparent, lower-cost official bonds — a balance-sheet cleanup, not a "buy back debt to reduce it" operation like the US Treasury's liquidity buybacks. Different problem (opacity/rollover risk at the local level), different tool (formal debt-for-bond swaps).

4.5 Growth & stimulus watch

Q2 2026 GDP growth ~4.3%, Q3 tracking ~4% (bank estimates, no official print yet) — below Beijing's own 4.5–5% target, the lowest formal target on record. Analysts flag late-Sept/early-Oct as a plausible stimulus window if growth prints at or below 4%, with a reserve-requirement-ratio cut seen as more likely than an outright rate cut — Beijing has room to ease without inflation risk, since it's fighting deflation, not inflation.

05

United States the hawkish pivot

The market that moved the most since your notes were written — lead with the Fed, not the AI story, if asked to open here.

5.1 Jobs, inflation, and the Fed's actual decision

  • August jobs (rel. 4 Sep): nonfarm payrolls +162,000 vs. ~53,000 consensus — a big beat. Unemployment held at 4.1%. Average hourly earnings +0.3% m/m.
  • August CPI (rel. 11 Sep): headline +0.4% m/m / 3.4% y/y; core +0.3% m/m / 2.4% y/y — hotter than the 0.2% expected, driven by non-housing services (strongest monthly gain since January) and a 27.4% y/y jump in gasoline.
  • Kevin Warsh, Fed Chair, Jackson Hole (28 Aug): explicitly hawkish — flagged inflation as too high, argued for a "quieter" (more data-driven, less forward-guidance-dependent) Fed, opened the door to hikes. Hike odds for September jumped ~55% → ~60% on the speech alone.
  • FOMC, 16 Sep: hiked 25bp to 3.75–4.00%, 12-0, first hike since 2023. Cited resilient spending, strong productivity, robust capex, a labour market keeping pace with the workforce — alongside inflation still above target.
  • What's priced next: dot plot points to one more 25bp hike in 2026 (to 4.00–4.25%); next FOMC 28 Oct, then 9 Dec.

5.2 Treasury market — buybacks and the deficit

Separately from Fed policy, the Treasury tripled its long-bond buyback size (10–20Y and 20–30Y sectors) from $2bn to up to $6bn per operation, mid-August to early September, "for liquidity support." Context: July's fiscal deficit was $432.3bn (highest since March 2021), pushing the YTD shortfall to ~$1.8 trillion against ~$40 trillion total debt and ~$1.2 trillion in annual interest costs — and foreign demand is fading, with China and Japan both trimming UST holdings. The 30-year sits near a 19-year high (~5.2–5.3%). This is the "who buys the debt when foreigners won't" problem in miniature.

5.3 Talking points, cold

  • The AI capex cycle (hyperscaler data-centre spend) remains the single biggest driver of US equity outperformance vs. every other market here — a large part of why the S&P is up ~5% YTD while the Nifty is down ~7%.
  • The dollar is structurally firmer post-hike — the transmission mechanism pressuring the rupee, and the reason gold/silver corrected hard from their January peaks.
  • Don't overstate a 5-year dollar call — the honest answer is "near-term firmer on the hiking cycle; longer-term the de-dollarization/central-bank-gold-buying trend argues for gradual erosion of the dollar's reserve share." Different time horizons, not a contradiction.
06

Japan deflation to normalization

The history first — this is what actually gets tested — then what's live right now.

6.1 Why Japan got stuck

Japan's asset bubble (real estate and equities) burst in the early 1990s. What followed became a textbook liquidity trap: the BOJ hit the zero lower bound by 1995, and even free money couldn't get sustained inflation going, because the real constraint wasn't borrowing cost — households and firms, scarred by the bubble collapse, wouldn't spend or invest even at zero rates. Deflation persisted for most of the next 25 years.

Why deflation is self-reinforcing: falling prices raise the real value of existing debt — the yen amount owed doesn't change, but each yen buys more, so debtors deleverage instead of spending, and consumers rationally delay purchases because things will be cheaper next year. Both effects suppress demand further, pushing prices down more — a trap monetary policy alone struggles to break, since nominal rates can't be pushed meaningfully below zero to compensate.

6.2 The inflation Japan wanted vs. the inflation it got

Japan spent decades trying to engineer demand-pull inflation — the "good" kind, where rising wages and confident consumers bid up prices because they're spending more, self-sustaining and a sign of health. What actually broke the deflationary mindset in the 2020s was largely cost-push (imported) inflation: global commodity shocks and yen depreciation (from policy divergence with the Fed) made imported energy and goods pricier, pushing up prices from the cost side, compounded by a tight labour market from an aging, shrinking workforce. A meaningfully different, less comfortable kind of inflation — it raises living costs without necessarily reflecting stronger domestic demand, exactly the tension the BOJ now navigates while hiking.

6.3 What's happening now

  • Policy path: 0.75% → 1.0% (June 2026) → 1.25% (mid-Sep 2026, highest since 1995), a 7-2 split vote — genuine internal disagreement on pace.
  • Why hike now: core inflation near 2.8–3%, above target long enough for the BOJ to judge the reflation case at least partly achieved — even if some of it is cost-push per above.
  • The counterintuitive reaction: after the September hike, the yen weakened, JGB yields fell, and the Nikkei rallied — the opposite of the textbook script. Read: the hike was already priced in, so it became "sell the news" on yields, with focus shifting to how gradual the BOJ will be from here — gradual tightening is a green light for risk assets and keeps yen-funded carry trades only partly unwound.
  • Nikkei: closed ~66,364 on 25 Sep, having touched a fresh high near 67,330 on 22–23 Sep — domestic, non-exporter names leading, consistent with "gradual normalization, improving domestic demand" rather than an export-driven rally.
  • Yen outlook: intervention chatter rising (early September) even amid hikes; medium-term, continued gradual tightening should narrow the Japan–US rate gap and support a stronger yen — pace genuinely contested inside the BOJ (next hike watched for December).

7.2 Gold & silver — the fuller picture

2025 into early 2026 was a genuine structural bull market: gold's all-time high $5,589 (28 Jan 2026), silver's $121.62 (29 Jan 2026, up ~170% from $38 in July 2025) — structural drivers, not speculation alone:

  • Central-bank buying / de-dollarization: since Russia's ~$300bn in FX reserves were frozen in 2022, central banks (especially BRICS+, now 17.4% of global gold reserves vs. 11.2% in 2019) have diversified reserves away from dollar assets into gold at record pace (~60 tonnes/month average cited for 2026).
  • Silver-specific squeeze: a sixth consecutive year of structural deficit (100–200moz/year shortfall), a COMEX vault drawdown (>33moz in one January week), new AI/electronics industrial demand, and China reclassifying silver as strategic and restricting exports.

Since then both corrected hard — gold to $4,298 (−23%), silver to $64.2 (−47%) — as the Warsh-led hawkish pivot and the September hike pushed real yields up and the dollar firmer (§2). Frame it as: the structural, multi-year bid is intact; the cyclical driver (real rates, Fed path) just turned against the trade hard enough to dominate short-term. The defensible trade idea is relative-value, not directional: if the hiking cycle is close to done (one more priced), the structural bid should reassert once the rate shock is absorbed — a laddered, scaled re-entry beats chasing the correction or calling an outright fresh long right now.

Cheat sheet

Likely questions, tight answers.

What's your read on the Indian economy right now?
Structurally strong, cyclically squeezed: inflation at 4.82% (3rd month above target, monsoon-driven), rupee at a record weak ~96/USD, 10Y G-sec just broke 7% on RBI liquidity draining after the FCNR(B) inflow — but the government just cut its FY27 borrowing programme by ₹1.2 lakh crore on stronger tax and RBI-dividend revenue, which pulls the other way on yields. Two forces on the same curve, not one clean story. Nifty is cheap on a 10-year basis (~19.7x) but still saw outflows because India has no seat at the AI/data-centre table driving the US, Taiwan and Korea higher.
Give me a trade idea.
Long PFC as a levered proxy on India's power-capex buildout (AI data-centres, ~8GW capacity by 2030), hedged against rate risk via a short in rate-sensitive NBFCs or India rate futures, sized alongside Power Grid to diversify financing-risk vs. transmission-toll-booth risk.
Walk me through how a hawkish Fed speech moves gold.
Speech repriced hike odds up → real yields rise → opportunity cost of holding non-yielding gold rises → gold sells off; the dollar firms on the same repricing, independently pressuring gold (dollar-priced) and EM currencies like the rupee. We watched this exact chain: Warsh's 28 Aug speech, the 16 Sep hike, gold falling from its January highs.
Why is a smaller government borrowing programme good for markets?
Less competition for savings against corporate borrowers and banks — G-sec yields are the risk-free base every other rate in the economy is priced off, so less supply eases the whole curve and cheapens private financing (less "crowding out"). Caveat: this matters most when the economy is near full capacity; with slack, government borrowing can be absorbed without much yield impact, and public capex can crowd private investment in instead.
Does lower government borrowing strengthen the rupee?
Mainly through credibility — a lower deficit improves fiscal optics and the sovereign risk premium, which is currency-supportive. The direct mechanical link is weaker than people assume, because India's G-sec market is domestically funded, not externally — and lower yields from less bond supply can slightly reduce FPI debt inflows (lower carry), which cuts the other way. Net: supportive, but through credibility and growth, not a straight-line mechanism.
What just happened with UPI, and does it matter?
From 15 Oct, a 0.4% MDR applies to P2M (merchant) UPI transactions above ₹2,000, capped at ₹300 for ₹75,000+ — P2P transfers stay free. Small point in growth terms, but a clean example of the government re-pricing a subsidised public rail rather than running it at zero revenue indefinitely — same instinct as the borrowing cut and the redesigned urea policy.
Compare China and India's currencies right now.
Same global backdrop (firm dollar, hawkish Fed) — opposite outcomes, because the underlying balance of payments is opposite. China: large trade/current-account surplus → analysts expect continued yuan strength (toward 6.65–6.90). India: twin-deficit pressure plus FPI outflows → rupee at record weakness (~96). A balance-of-payments story, not a "which central bank is better" story.
What's China's core economic problem?
Domestic demand is too weak to absorb its own production, so growth leans almost entirely on exports (record $1.19tn surplus, ~1/3 of 2025 GDP growth) — fragile because it invites tariff retaliation, and compatible with domestic deflation (10 straight quarters) even as headline growth holds, because factories keep producing at export scale while nobody at home is buying.
